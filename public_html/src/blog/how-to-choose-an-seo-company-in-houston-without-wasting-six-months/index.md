Good SEO hires are defensive decisions: you want to avoid wasted months and money while you confirm an agency can actually move calls and estimate requests. Start by insisting on access, a clear first-90-day plan, and reporting tied to real lead actions — not vanity metrics.

A short inspection up front saves time. Ask for proof the agency will: get access to your tools, show what’s broken, fix the clearest blockers, and improve one revenue-driving page quickly. If they resist those steps, consider it a red flag.

## Quick Answer

Before you sign anything, make the agency show you these basics:

- They request access: Google Search Console, Analytics, Google Business Profile, and website/CMS.
- A Free Competitor & Keyword Report (or similar) that identifies which searches your competitors win.
- A concrete 90-day starting plan with prioritized pages, tracking, and one early revenue page to improve.
- Monthly reporting tied to calls, forms, and estimate requests — not just rankings or traffic.
- A clear cancellation or month-to-month path after an initial trial period.

## A simple decision framework (risk-first)

1. Inspect: Give short-term access so the agency can see your real data.
2. Baseline: Ask for a short baseline report showing current leads, pages that convert, and obvious indexing problems.
3. Prioritize: Require a prioritized list of pages and quick technical fixes tied to revenue.
4. Deliver: Expect at least one clear, visible improvement in the first 30–90 days (tracking fixed, page updated, or published).
5. Measure: Reports must show the connection between search visibility and calls/forms.
6. Continue or stop: If the first phase is vague or invisible, walk away.

This sequence keeps risk low. If an agency can’t follow it, they will likely waste your time.

## The 30–90 day proof checklist (use this on a call)

- Access requested: Search Console, Analytics, Google Business Profile, CMS/admin access.
- Baseline report: current calls, forms, and estimate requests tied to organic search.
- Competitor & keyword snapshot: who ranks for your profitable service searches in Houston.
- Technical quick-check: crawl/indexation, obvious broken pages, and site speed blockers.
- Tracking setup: phone-call tracking, form tracking, and event goals in Analytics.
- One prioritized service page: rewrite or publish a page directly tied to revenue.
- Internal linking plan: show which supporting pages will boost the target page.
- Content plan: topic list prioritized by search intent and revenue relevance.
- Publishing plan: who publishes and how often (you or the agency).
- Weekly communications: short updates on work completed and next actions.

If the agency can’t produce this checklist as a concrete plan, it’s a warning.

## What a realistic first-90-day deliverable looks like (sample)

- Free Competitor & Keyword Report with 6–12 missed searches and recommended pages.
- Tracking confirmation: Search Console and Analytics connected, call/form tracking enabled.
- 90-day priority list: 3 service pages to update or create and 4 supporting articles.
- One published revenue page (or one page with agency-published edits) and visible URL.
- Weekly one-page progress notes and a month-end report showing actions and lead evidence.

## Red flags to stop the conversation

- No request for access to Search Console or Analytics.
- Vague scope: "we’ll do some SEO" without named pages or tasks.
- Commodity page promises: a fixed number of posts per month with no market research.
- Reports that show only rankings or broad traffic numbers with no calls/forms data.
- Long lock-in contracts without a short, measurable proof period.

If you hear any of those, push for the 90-day checklist. If the agency resists, move on.

## How to judge progress without vanity metrics

Ask the agency to show these pieces each month:

- Which pages were created or improved and why they matter.
- Changes to tracking and the raw call/form data for the month.
- Which specific searches started showing impressions or clicks for target pages.
- Work completed list (technical fixes, published pages, internal link changes).

If reports don’t include calls, forms, and the actual page edits completed, the report is mostly noise.
